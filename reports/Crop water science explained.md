# How AgroPilot turns weather into drip minutes

AgroPilot's daily plan is a chain of five conversions: weather becomes a reference water demand (ET0), a crop factor (Kc) turns that into the crop's own use (ETc), a "soil bucket" tracks what is left in the root zone, a trigger rule decides when to refill it, and a unit conversion turns the refill depth into litres and drip run minutes. Each link follows the FAO-56 method (the UN Food and Agriculture Organization's Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56, the standard reference for this calculation), but the project departs from FAO or from typical drip practice in three places that matter. First, the "irrigate when the level would fall below half" rule is the project's own choice, not an FAO value; FAO's no-stress limits for these crops are tighter (40% depletion for tomato, 30% for bell pepper at about 5 mm/day). Second, refilling the whole bucket in one event is the FAO Annex 8 surface-irrigation pattern, whereas drip guidance is small, frequent applications. Third, together these produce events of tens of millimetres and run times that, at a 0.6 L/h emitter on 1.5 m x 0.4 m spacing (1.0 mm/h), can exceed 24 hours: refilling the 70 mm bucket in the worked example takes 42 hours. The arithmetic of the chain is sound and the worked numbers below reproduce; the agronomic fit to drip is the weak point. Note on evidence: the research notes read the FAO pages through a summarising fetch tool rather than raw text, so FAO wording is quoted as relayed, and the notes flag specific unverified items, which are called out where they arise.

## ET0 is the weather's thirst, measured on imaginary grass

Evaporation is liquid water turning to vapour from a surface such as soil or wet leaves; transpiration is the same change inside plant leaves, with vapour escaping through tiny leaf openings called stomata. FAO combines them as evapotranspiration (ET) because they happen together and "there is no easy way of distinguishing between the two processes" ([FAO-56 Ch. 1](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e04.htm)). The balance shifts over a season: at sowing nearly 100% of ET is soil evaporation, while at full crop cover more than 90% is transpiration ([FAO-56 Ch. 1](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e04.htm)). That is why a tomato seedling on bare soil uses little water and a full canopy uses a lot.

ET0 ("reference evapotranspiration") is the ET rate of a hypothetical, well-watered grass surface 0.12 m tall, with a fixed surface resistance of 70 s/m and an albedo (reflectivity) of 0.23 ([FAO-56 Ch. 2](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e06.htm)). Think of it as a standard ruler for how thirsty the weather is. Because it depends only on climate, "it obviates the need to define a separate ET level for each crop and stage of growth" and values compare directly across places and seasons ([FAO-56 Ch. 1](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e04.htm)). Four weather inputs drive it: solar radiation (the energy that vaporises water, about 2.45 MJ per square metre per millimetre), air temperature, humidity and wind speed ([FAO-56 Ch. 1](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e04.htm)). A hot, dry, windy, sunny day pulls hardest because all four push the same way. FAO does not rank the inputs; calling radiation the dominant one is the notes' inference, not a cited claim. Rain is not an input at all.

Typical values run about 1-3 mm/day on cool days, 4-7 mm/day in summer, and up to 8-10 mm/day in hot arid conditions, with summer demand 4-5 times winter demand: Yuma, Arizona, sees roughly 7.6-10.2 mm/day in summer and below 2.5 mm/day in winter ([FAO-56 Ch. 1, Table 2](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e04.htm); [University of Arizona Extension](https://www.extension.arizona.edu/publication/daily-reference-evapotranspiration-eto-yuma-valley-what-seasonal-pattern-means)). AgroPilot takes its daily ET0 from Open-Meteo's `et0_fao_evapotranspiration`, the FAO-56 Penman-Monteith value for well-watered grass, summed from 24 hourly values in millimetres ([Open-Meteo docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs)). Two honest limits apply. It assumes unlimited soil water, so it is potential demand, not actual loss from dry soil. And it is computed from gridded weather-model data, up to 2 km resolution ([Open-Meteo blog](https://openmeteo.substack.com/p/reference-evapotranspiration-for)), so it estimates a grid cell, not your plot's microclimate; the notes found no validation statistics for Open-Meteo's implementation.

One unit makes the rest of the report easy: **1 mm of water over 1 square metre is exactly 1 litre** (1 m2 x 0.001 m = 0.001 m3). FAO states the equivalent per hectare, 1 mm = 10 m3/ha ([FAO-56 Ch. 1](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e04.htm)); the litre-per-square-metre sentence itself is arithmetic the notes could not find printed in FAO-56. So a 1,000 m2 plot with ET0 of 5 mm/day has 5,000 L of reference demand per day, and the same millimetre unit works for rain, crop use and irrigation alike.

## Kc and growth stages convert grass demand into tomato and pepper use

The crop coefficient Kc is a dimensionless factor, and crop water use under ideal conditions is **ETc = Kc x ET0** ([FAO-56 Ch. 5](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0a.htm)). It works because ET0 already carries the weather effect, so Kc depends mainly on the crop: its height, leaf area and canopy resistance, how much ground it shades, and how much water evaporates from bare soil. A seedling on mostly bare soil has a low Kc (soil evaporation alone can give about 0.1 when dry); a full leafy canopy exceeds 1 because it is taller and rougher than grass.

FAO splits the season into four growth stages: initial (planting to about 10% ground cover, Kc ini, constant), development (cover rising to effective full cover, Kc ramps up), mid-season (full cover to the start of maturity, Kc mid, the maximum, constant) and late season (maturity to harvest, Kc falls to Kc end) ([FAO-56 Ch. 5](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0a.htm); [Ch. 6](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0b.htm)). The curve is three values joined by straight lines, with interpolation Kc = Kc prev + [(day - days in earlier stages) / stage length] x (Kc next - Kc prev) (Eq. 66, read from the equation image in the notes).

The project's tomato uses stages of 30/40/45/30 days (145 total; FAO Table 11, Mediterranean, April/May planting) and Kc 0.6/1.15/0.80 ([FAO-56 Ch. 6, Tables 11-12](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0b.htm)). **On day 50** the crop is 20 days into the 40-day development stage, so Kc = 0.6 + (20/40) x (1.15 - 0.6) = **0.875**. With a daily ET0 of 5.0 mm, ETc = 0.875 x 5.0 = **4.375 mm/day**. On day 20 (initial stage) ETc would be 0.6 x 5 = 3.0 mm; on day 90 (mid-season) it peaks at 1.15 x 5 = 5.75 mm; on day 130 Kc has slid to 0.975. Bell pepper uses 30/35/40/20 days and Kc 0.6/1.05/0.90 (Table 12 Kc mid 1.05, Kc end 0.90).

The notes document where the project's numbers rest on judgment rather than a direct table entry. FAO leaves the Kc ini cells blank for tomato and pepper, so the 0.6 comes from the Solanum-family group row. Tomato Kc end is printed as a range of 0.70-0.90, and 0.80 is the group value and midpoint. Pepper's initial stage is printed "25/30" with a total of 125, which only sums if 30 is used; FAO does not explain the slash. All of these are supported, not errors. FAO also says stage lengths are "useful only as a general guide" and local observations should replace them ([FAO-56 Ch. 6](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0b.htm)).

Two FAO refinements are deliberately skipped by the project. The wind/humidity adjustment (Eq. 62) is small for short crops: for a windy, dry tomato season (wind 3 m/s, minimum humidity 35%) it lifts Kc mid only from 1.15 to about 1.20. More important for drip, FAO says that when only a fraction fw of the soil surface is wetted (0.3-0.4 for trickle), Kc ini is multiplied by fw ([FAO-56 Ch. 6](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0b.htm), Example 26; fw values per [Ch. 7, Table 20](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0c.htm), the latter via a summary only). Applied to 0.6 that gives 0.18-0.24, so the project's early-season ETc is probably overstated for drip, while FAO's footnote notes Kc ini can instead rise toward 1.0-1.2 with very frequent wetting. Finally, the project uses a single Kc, which FAO says suits planning and less frequent irrigation, whereas a dual coefficient (Kcb + Ke, splitting transpiration from soil evaporation) is recommended for daily, high-frequency scheduling ([FAO-56 Ch. 5](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0a.htm); [Ch. 7](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0c.htm)). The single-Kc planner is a simplification FAO itself would flag for drip.

## The soil bucket holds 70 mm and the half rule empties it past FAO's stress point

Soil holds usable water between two limits. Field capacity (FC) is what a well-drained soil retains once gravity drainage has largely stopped; the permanent wilting point (WP) is where plants can no longer extract water. The difference is plant-available water, typically 50-110 mm per metre of depth for sand and 130-180 mm/m for loam ([FAO-56 Ch. 7, Table 19](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0c.htm), via summary). Plants only drink from the soil their roots reach, so the bucket size is **TAW = available water per metre x root depth** (total available water, FAO Eq. 82; [FAO-56 Ch. 8](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0e.htm)). The project fixes root depth at 0.7 m for tomato and 0.5 m for bell pepper, the low ends of FAO's 0.7-1.5 m and 0.5-1.0 m ranges, which FAO says suit scheduling ([FAO-56 Ch. 8, Table 22](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0e.htm)). With the registry's soil values (sand 83, loam 175, clay loam 200 mm/m, taken from the project skill file and not re-checked against registry code), a tomato bucket is 58.1 mm in sand, 122.5 mm in loam and 140 mm in clay loam. Clay loam's 200 mm/m sits above FAO's 130-180 range for the nearest table row, plausible but not an FAO "typical" value. The notes' illustrative **70 mm bucket** is, for example, 100 mm/m over 0.7 m.

Depletion (Dr) is the empty space at the top of the bucket, measured from field capacity, so level = TAW - Dr. Each day rain refills the level and ETc drains it; anything above full drains away the same day ([FAO-56 Ch. 8, Eqs. 85-88](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0e.htm)). The project's level form is L_pre = min(TAW, L_prev + rain - ETc), starting the first day full. Yesterday's rain still counts because the level carries forward: 40 mm in the bucket plus 12 mm rain minus 5.75 mm ETc leaves 46.25 mm, and no irrigation is needed. The project ignores runoff, capillary rise and FAO's rule that daily rain under about 0.2 ET0 can be ignored.

Plants are not stressed the moment the bucket starts to empty. FAO defines readily available water, **RAW = p x TAW**, as the part that can be used before stress begins, where p is the allowed depleted fraction; beyond it, ET falls in proportion to the water left ([FAO-56 Ch. 8](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0e.htm)). FAO's Table 22 gives **p = 0.40 for tomato and 0.30 for bell pepper** at ETc of about 5 mm/day, adjusted by p = p(table) + 0.04 x (5 - ETc), so hot days lower it. FAO notes 0.50 is "commonly used for many crops" but does not give it for these two. The project's trigger, **irrigate when the level would fall below 0.5 x TAW** for both crops, with exactly half not triggering, comes from the task specification, per the notes; its rationale is not documented.

The worked example shows what this does. Take the 70 mm bucket, full, losing 7 mm/day with no rain; the trigger is 35 mm. The level reads 63, 56, 49, 42 and 35 mm at the end of days 1-5; exactly 35 does not trigger. On day 6 the level would be 28 mm, below 35, so the planner irrigates 70 - 28 = **42 mm** and restores 70 mm. Compare FAO: at ETc 7 mm/day, tomato p = 0.40 + 0.04 x (5 - 7) = 0.32, so RAW = 22.4 mm, and stress begins once depletion exceeds 22.4 mm, i.e. level under 47.6 mm. That happens on day 4 (level 42, depletion 28), two days before the project irrigates. FAO's stress coefficient Ks = (TAW - Dr) / (TAW - RAW) would then be (70 - 28)/(70 - 22.4) = 0.88, meaning the crop would use about 88% of its normal water. At a calmer ETc of 5, FAO's limit is a level of 60% for tomato and 70% for bell pepper, so the half rule lets pepper run 20 percentage points past FAO's no-stress point. The half rule is therefore not "safe" by FAO's numbers; it knowingly accepts mild stress, and the project applies no Ks, so its plan assumes the crop still uses full ETc below the RAW point. Whether that stress is acceptable is a project decision the notes could not verify. Also keep the two framings straight: FAO's p is depletion allowed, the project's 0.5 is level allowed to fall to; they coincide only at one half.

## From millimetres to litres to minutes, and why 42 mm on drip is a problem

The conversion is two steps. Depth times area gives litres: 42 mm on a 200 m2 plot is 42 x 200 = **8,400 L**. Run time comes from the drip line's application rate: emitter flow (L/h) divided by the soil area each emitter serves (row spacing x emitter spacing) gives mm/h, and run time is depth divided by rate. With 0.6 L/h emitters on 1.5 m x 0.4 m spacing, each serves 0.60 m2, so the rate is 0.6/0.60 = **1.0 mm/h**. This matches how practical drip calculators work ([Toro](https://driptips.toro.com/drip-irrigation-calculator/), from a search snippet) and the extension method of dividing daily need by system rate ([UF/IFAS AE500](https://journals.flvc.org/edis/article/download/131527/135108/236407)). Check by emitters: 200 m2 / 0.6 m2 = 333 emitters x 0.6 L/h = 200 L/h, and 8,400 L / 200 L/h = 42 h, consistent. Net requirement is what the root zone must receive; the system must deliver gross = net / application efficiency, typically about 90% for drip (range 80-95%) ([UF/IFAS AE500](https://journals.flvc.org/edis/article/download/131527/135108/236407)). Using the day-50 tomato example, 4.375 mm of daily ETc takes 4.4 h (262 min) at 1.0 mm/h, or about 4.9 h (292 min) at 90% efficiency. The notes did not examine whether the planner itself applies an efficiency factor.

Now the mismatch. A 30 mm refill takes 30 h net at 1.0 mm/h (33 h gross), and 42 mm takes 42 h; at the notes' alternative 1.0 L/h emitter (1.67 mm/h) the figures are 18 h and about 25 h. A 60 mm event needs 60 h at 1.0 mm/h. Anything above 24 h cannot run in a day at all. This is not just an inconvenience; it signals that the refill rule is in conflict with drip physics. Drip wets only a fraction fw (0.3-0.4) of the surface, so a field-average 30-60 mm means roughly 75-200 mm in the wetted strip (the notes' inference), far above what that small root volume holds (the notes' illustrative estimate is 16-24 mm, an unsourced figure built from an AE500 example). Extension guidance points the other way: FAO's drip manual describes "small, frequent, precise quantities", with depletion not exceeding 40% late in the season and 20-30% early, and daily irrigation common in late stages ([FAO Irrigation Manual Ch. 14](http://www.fao.org/4/a1336e/a1336e14a.pdf)); UC ANR advises 20-30% depletion and roughly every other day at full canopy ([UC ANR](https://ucanr.edu/sites/default/files/2011-02/75715.pdf)); and UF/IFAS sizes single cycles to the wetted bulb, about 0.5 mm per cycle, splitting 3.3 mm of daily need into 9 cycles ([UF/IFAS AE500](https://journals.flvc.org/edis/article/download/131527/135108/236407)), warning that longer cycles leach water and nutrients past the roots. Annex 8, the FAO example the project's skeleton resembles, does irrigate "exactly equal to the depletion depth" the morning after the depletion limit is reached ([FAO-56 Annex 8](http://www.fao.org/4/x0490E/x0490e0p.htm)); but it is a dry-bean dual-Kc demonstration with fw 0.5 and says nothing about system capacity (the notes read it second-hand, and its Box 8.1 formulas were not read). Refilling to field capacity is thus defensible as bookkeeping for a whole-plot bucket, not as a drip event size.

## Conclusion

The science chain is coherent and the numbers reproduce: ET0 sets the weather's demand, Kc 0.875 on tomato day 50 makes that 4.375 mm of crop use at ET0 5, the bucket converts use into a level, and 1 mm = 1 L/m2 converts the answer into litres and, via 1.0 mm/h, into hours. The weak link is not any one formula but the stacking of three simplifications, each defensible alone: a looser-than-FAO trigger, a refill to full, and a single Kc that ignores partial wetting. Together they plan stress-prone, 30-60 mm, 30-60 hour events that no drip system can run in a day and that its small wetted root volume could not hold.

The notes point to a pragmatic fix that preserves the existing bucket: keep it, cap each event near the wetted-zone capacity, irrigate daily or several times per week with the run time reported in hours, and flag any plan above 24 hours per day as infeasible. Alternatives found in the sources are daily irrigation of ETc divided by efficiency, soil-moisture triggers at 20-40% depletion, or sizing the bucket by wetted volume as UC does (rooting depth x available water x fraction wetted x allowed depletion). Open questions remain for the project owner: why 0.5 was chosen, whether efficiency and fw are meant to apply, and whether the stress tolerance of the half rule is intended. No source evaluated a refill-at-50% drip plan for vegetables directly, so that conclusion rests on the combined guidance above rather than on a single study.
